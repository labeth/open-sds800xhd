open-sds800xhd — SAFETY / USE AT YOUR OWN RISK
===============================================

This is EXPERIMENTAL clean-room replacement firmware for the Siglent SDS800X HD
series oscilloscope (SDS802X HD to SDS824X HD). It has run in a simulator only:
NOTHING HERE HAS RUN ON A REAL UNIT YET.

- Once installed, it TAKES OVER the instrument at every boot: a couple of
  seconds after power-on it stops the vendor application and runs this
  firmware instead.
- It drives the real hardware (relays, gain/offset DACs, ADCs, the
  programmable logic's registers). A bug can wedge the acquisition engine or
  leave the front end in an unexpected state. The unit has no watchdog: a hang
  stays a hang until you switch the mains off.
- It is for the SDS800X HD SERIES ONLY. Do not run it on other hardware.
- Its network services (web UI, SCPI over VXI-11, ports 5024/5025) have NO
  AUTHENTICATION. Use it on a trusted network only.
- There is NO WARRANTY. You are responsible for your instrument. See LICENSE.


Why the unit stays recoverable: the rules the firmware never breaks
--------------------------------------------------------------------

At every boot, before Linux starts, the vendor U-Boot looks for a USB stick
carrying a recovery environment (huluwa_udiskEnv.txt). That stick route is
how the vendor recovers a unit, how this firmware is installed and how it is
removed. U-Boot lives in BOOT.bin on eMMC partition p1. As long as nothing
touches what that route depends on, a unit can always be brought back.

So this firmware writes ONLY under p12's root, /usr/bin/siglent/usr:
extra.sh (the launch hook), open/... (the app's slots, logs, settings and
setups) and the user self-calibration file. Captures go to a USB stick, never
to the eMMC. It NEVER:

  1. writes p1 (BOOT.bin: FSBL, boot PL image, U-Boot; uEnv.txt), p5 (kernel
     Image, system.dtb), p7 or p10 (the vendor system), or any raw eMMC block
     device (/dev/mmcblk0, its partitions or boot areas);
  2. creates or writes a U-Boot environment: no uboot.env on any partition,
     no fw_setenv / saveenv. (A valid uboot.env would replace U-Boot's boot
     command, including the stick check, for good.)
  3. touches the Zynq eFuses, the BBRAM key or any secure-boot or key storage
     (nvmem and eFuse files in /sys, the CSU, eFuse and BBRAM registers);
  4. writes p9, the factory data: calibration, serial number, MAC address,
     licences. It is read only. Nothing can recreate it.
  5. drives a PL pin whose function on this board is not verified (below);
  6. puts anything in p12's updatefile/ or usr/upgrade/ directories: the
     vendor boot flashes what it finds there to p1, p5, p7, p9 and p10.

These rules are enforced in code, not only by convention. Every file write,
rename and delete of the supervisor, the installer, the census and the app
goes through one write guard (open-dso-core's fsguard with this unit's
policy, app/hdguard). It refuses, with an error that says why, any path
outside p12's root after resolving ".." and symlinks, any path through a
symlink on p12 at all, the two vendor update directories and any root in
or resolving into them, any uboot.env, BOOT.bin or uEnv.txt, block devices,
eFuse/BBRAM/nvmem paths, and /dev/mem windows over the CSU, eFuse, BBRAM and
SD/eMMC controllers. The launch hook, a shell script outside the guard,
tests every path it writes with [ -L ] first: it writes nothing through a
symlink, and does nothing at all when open/ itself is one. The install
stick's /init writes only after the kernel's mount table shows
/dev/mmcblk0p12 (ext4) on its mount point and no other eMMC partition
mounted writable. The census writes nothing to the eMMC at all: its report
goes to the stick or to RAM.


The programmable logic (PL): why a wrong pin map is a hardware risk
-------------------------------------------------------------------

Today the firmware keeps the vendor's PL image. An open PL image (fpga/) is
being designed. A PL image decides which FPGA balls are outputs. If our map of
the board is wrong and an image drives a ball that is in fact an output of
another chip, or tied to a rail, the two drivers fight; that can damage the
FPGA or the other part, and no stick or reflash repairs that.

Every board fact the open image depends on is listed in spec 20 §14 and in
fpga/vivado/verified.tcl, each 0 (unverified) or 1 (measured on a unit and
written into the specs). The guards:

- The build refuses to make a bitstream while any fact is 0
  (fpga/vivado/build.tcl bitstream). All are 0 today: no open PL image for
  the unit exists.
- Each image is built with a manifest (open_pl.json) recording the facts it
  was built against and its sha256. The board loads an image through
  fpga_manager only when that manifest declares every fact verified and the
  sha256 matches (app/internal/plimage); otherwise nothing reaches the PL.
- A bring-up or test image, when one is made to settle facts, keeps every
  ball whose function is still unverified as an input.
- A bad PL image is undone by a power cycle: the FSBL loads the vendor PL
  image from BOOT.bin at every boot, and BOOT.bin is never written.


Getting back to the vendor firmware
-----------------------------------

From mildest to strongest:

1. Next boot only: hold the Acquire key while you switch the unit on, and
   keep it held until the vendor application's screen appears: the launch
   hook reads it late in the boot, before it starts anything, and leaves that one boot to the vendor
   firmware; nothing is written, and the next boot runs ours again. Acquire
   was chosen because in the vendor firmware it only opens a menu: the vendor
   firmware seeing it held changes no setting.
2. Next boot and every boot after: choose the vendor firmware, without
   uninstalling anything. Any of
     - SCPI:      :SYSTem:BOOT VENDOR   (:SYSTem:BOOT OPEN undoes it,
                  :SYSTem:BOOT? says which)
     - web:       POST /api/boot with "vendor" or "open"; GET /api/boot
     - otactl:    otactl vendor [reboot]  /  otactl open
   writes or removes /usr/bin/siglent/usr/open/disable. While that file
   exists the launch hook exits at once and the vendor firmware runs
   untouched. The supervisor also creates it by itself after 3 reboots in a
   row without a healthy app, so a firmware that keeps crashing ends up
   leaving the unit to the vendor firmware at the next power-up. Removing it
   (OPEN, otactl open, or deleting the file) runs ours again.
3. Uninstall stick. Boot with the uninstall stick inserted: it deletes
   extra.sh and the install markers on p12 and reboots into the vendor
   firmware. The rest of open/ stays (harmless without extra.sh); a later
   install stick overwrites it.
4. Remove extra.sh. Deleting /usr/bin/siglent/usr/extra.sh by any means (for
   example from a shell on the serial console) returns the unit to the vendor
   firmware at the next boot. Nothing else of ours runs at boot.
5. Vendor recovery stick (spec 01 §11.3). It rewrites p1, p5, p7, p8 and p10
   from the vendor's recovery package. Use only the package for your unit's
   firmware and hardware version. WARNING: a recovery stick that carries
   firmdata0.ext4.gz OVERWRITES p9 (calibration, serial, MAC, licences). NEVER
   use a stick carrying another unit's firmdata0.ext4.gz: your unit's
   factory data would be gone for good. The vendor package does not carry
   it; check before you boot.

After any recovery boot, remove the stick: U-Boot runs a stick's environment
at every boot while it is inserted.


First contact with a new unit: the staged order
-----------------------------------------------

Nothing of ours writes to the eMMC until the unit's own data is backed up and
the way back is proven. In order, stopping at the first unexpected result:

1. Passive and read-only: photographs, the vendor UI's version page, network
   queries, the serial console's boot log. No code of ours on the unit.
2. Census and backup stick (our first code on the unit). It boots the vendor
   kernel with our initramfs from the stick, mounts nothing of the eMMC for
   writing, and saves the census and raw backups of every partition (p9 and
   p11 above all), with their SHA-256, to the stick. Keep two copies of the
   backups off the unit; they hold your serial, MAC, calibration and licences,
   so keep them private.
3. Prove the way back before the first install: install, uninstall and
   restore sticks rehearsed against an image of the unit's own dumps in the
   emulator; a restore stick that writes back p12 from its own backup; who
   switches the mains when it hangs.
4. Only then the install stick. It writes extra.sh and open/... on p12 and
   nothing else.
